# NPLM -- Under Construction
NPLM's or Neural Probabilistic Language Models are the predecessors to the modern large language model and a fascinating facet of the history
of statistical models that learn about language. Language models aim to model the joint probability distribution of a sequence of words.

$$
P(x_1, x_2 \ldots, x_T)
= P(x_1) \prod_{t=2}^{T} P(x_t \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1})
$$

The predecessor to the NPLM was n-grams, which are quite simple. They simply make the assumption that the current word is only dependent on the previous n - 1 words.
So for this assumption we model the probability of the sequence as follows.

$$
P(x_t \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1})
= P(x_t \mid x_{t -n + 1}, \ldots, x_{t-1})
$$

$$
P(x_1, x_2 \ldots, x_T)
= \prod_{t=1}^{T} P(x_t \mid x_{t - n + 1}, \ldots, x_{t-1})
$$

For a unigram this is a very simple case, we just approximate the probability of the next word given all the previous ones as the marginal probability of that word.
For a bigram we model the probability of the next word as the conditional probability of the next word given the previous one. 
The Maximum Likelihood Estimation for the probability of a sequence given this n-gram assumption is simply the count of the n words before the next word and the next word 
divided by the count of the n words before the next word, given a particular corpus.

In 2003, Bengio et al.[^1] discovered that using continuous representations of words and a neural model, that can leverage a competitive model that outperforms smoothed or 
interpolated n-gram models alone. These Neural Probabilistic Language Models, or NPLMs, leveraged a shared parameter lookup matrix (an embedding table for words), and a shallow
neural network to predict the probability of the next word given n-words of context. I wanted to dive deeper into it in this blog posting.
